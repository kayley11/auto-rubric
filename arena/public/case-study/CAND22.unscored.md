## Final answer, unchanged

**Clinical Summary**

A 14-year-old boy presents with intermittent burning pain in the hands and feet since approximately age four, worsened by heat and exertion, with persistence into adolescence. He also reports recurrent abdominal discomfort, reduced exercise tolerance, anhidrosis, and fatigue. Earlier complaints were attributed to functional causes. Maternal relatives have early kidney disease and cardiomyopathy of unknown aetiology; no confirmed familial diagnosis exists. Initial examination shows no angiokeratomas or corneal verticillata; cardiovascular, abdominal, and respiratory findings are normal. He is short in stature (155 cm, 37 kg) with reduced pinprick and vibration sensation in the lower limbs.

**Prioritised Explanations**

1. Hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy: The combination of early-onset burning pain in acral distribution, heat sensitivity, anhidrosis, reduced lower-limb sensation, and a maternal family history of unexplained renal and cardiac disease is consistent with a lysosomal storage or related metabolic neuropathy. Uncertainty: no biochemical or genetic confirmation is available; the absence of angiokeratomas does not exclude the diagnosis.

2. Functional or psychosomatic pain syndrome: Previously considered; however, the objective sensory deficit, anhidrosis, and family history make this less likely as the sole explanation.

3. Other inherited neuropathy or connective-tissue disorder: Short stature and reduced exercise tolerance broaden the differential, but no specific features point to a single alternative.

**Next Steps**

- Enzyme assay, lyso-GL-3 or equivalent biochemical testing, and genetic panel.
- Formal baseline organ screening (renal, cardiac, neurological).
- Nerve conduction studies.
- Cascade family screening once a familial variant is identified.
- Serial growth monitoring.

## Provisional reference

This boy has a prolonged childhood-onset pain syndrome with functional limitation that merits reassessment beyond the earlier functional label. Clarify the distribution, severity and chronology of burning pain, heat or exertional triggers, sweating, gastrointestinal symptoms and school or exercise impact using caregiver history and age-appropriate engagement with the child. Obtain the neurological examination and a careful pedigree rather than treating one symptom as diagnostic.

If received, reduced pinprick and vibration sensation provide objective sensory evidence, while low sweating, abdominal symptoms and heat intolerance suggest an autonomic component. Maternal relatives with early renal disease and cardiomyopathy raise concern for an inherited multisystem disorder, including an appropriate lysosomal or metabolic differential. Other causes of peripheral or small-fibre neuropathy remain possible and should be evaluated proportionately. Consanguinity alone neither proves the disease nor defines its inheritance.

Arrange paediatric neurology or metabolic assessment, suitable laboratory evaluation and targeted biochemical/genetic testing when the combined findings justify it. Assess potential renal, cardiac and other organ involvement with appropriate specialist input; normal initial examinations do not substitute for the later formal screening programme. Conversely, family disease does not establish organ damage in this boy. The absence of observed skin or corneal signs does not settle the diagnosis. Do not assert enzyme deficiency, a particular mutation, affected relatives’ confirmed results or treatment response before those investigations are available.
