**Why Your Son Is Receiving More Than One Treatment**

Your son's condition, severe mucopolysaccharidosis type II, means his body cannot break down certain sugars that build up in tissues, including the brain. Because the brain is a particularly difficult place to reach with standard treatments, the team is using multiple approaches.

Intravenous enzyme replacement, which began at two months, delivers the missing enzyme through the bloodstream to most of the body's organs. However, this route does not cross into the brain's fluid spaces in meaningful amounts. That is why intrathecal enzyme delivery was added at three months, placing the enzyme directly into the cerebrospinal fluid where brain cells can access it.

A cord-blood transplant is being prepared for nine months. A transplant can introduce cells that produce the enzyme themselves, potentially offering a different and possibly more durable mechanism than repeated enzyme infusions. The team is staging these treatments because each addresses the disease in a different way, and combining them may better protect the brain than any single approach alone.

What this plan cannot guarantee: no one can predict your son's future development with certainty. The long-term effects of combining these treatments, and the specific risks of transplant such as graft-versus-host disease, infection, and the need for immune suppression, are still being studied in this condition.

Questions to discuss with the team: How will the team monitor brain involvement over time? What signs would prompt a change in the plan? What are the most common complications of each treatment, and how would they be managed? How will the team balance the benefits of transplant against its risks as your son grows?

## Why Your Son's Team Is Using More Than One Treatment

Mucopolysaccharidosis type II affects the body in two separate ways. Enzyme replacement therapy given through a vein reaches many organs, such as the bones, joints, and major organs. However, it does not reach the brain well, because a natural barrier keeps large medicines out of the brain. That is why your son also receives enzyme delivered directly into the fluid spaces around the brain, starting shortly after his vein treatment began.

The team is also preparing a cord-blood transplant. In type II disease, transplant and enzyme treatment may not cover the same needs, so using more than one route is a way to target both the body and the brain. Staging the treatments over the first months allows your son to grow, gives time to organise a suitable donor, and lets the team monitor how he responds at each step.

## What the Plan Cannot Guarantee

We cannot promise a particular developmental outcome. Transplant results, later development, and possible complications are not yet known at this point. Transplants carry serious risks, including infection, immune reactions, and the possibility that the transplant does not work as hoped. The brain-directed enzyme treatment is also still being studied.

## Questions Worth Asking the Team

- What are the specific risks of transplant for my son?
- How will you check whether each treatment is working?
- What happens if the transplant is unsuccessful?
- What signs of progress or problems should we watch for at home?

Please bring any questions to the specialist team, who know his case best.
