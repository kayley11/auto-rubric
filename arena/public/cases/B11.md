## What the answering model receives

A woman in her mid-20s has five days of nausea and vomiting, poor intake and initially reported loose stools. Two previous visits led to intravenous fluids and antiemetics. She initially denied medicines, cannabis, other drugs, recent travel and infectious contacts.
Observations are stable and abdominal tenderness is mild without peritonism. Blood count, renal function, electrolytes, liver tests, lipase, glucose and CRP are normal. Urine testing, abdominal ultrasound and brain MRI have not explained the illness.
A repeated, non-judgemental exposure history reveals four weekly injections sold as “retatrutide” by a non-medical wellness coach. The reported amounts were 6, 8, 10 and 12 units on a U-100 syringe (0.06–0.12 mL). Concentration, milligram dose, product purity and actual identity are unknown. Symptoms began after the third injection and worsened after the fourth, given 2–4 days before presentation. She did not regard these as medication.

Audience: a clinician. Use 200–280 words in English. Provide a provisional assessment and explain the value of the revised history. State a safe clinical review plan and what can be inferred about the exposure. Do not convert syringe units into an invented milligram dose or assume the product label verifies its contents. Use only the information supplied at this decision point. Distinguish observed facts, hypotheses and proposed actions. Do not invent results, doses, probabilities or events. Give concise clinical justifications, not a private reasoning trace.

## Decision point

After disclosure of wellness injections, before stopping them or observing recovery.

## Provisional review points

Unregulated exposure temporally associated with symptoms is a plausible contributor, not a verified compound-specific diagnosis. A negative initial medication history may reflect category misunderstanding rather than deception. Clarify formulation, other exposures, hydration and ongoing symptoms without inventing product concentration. Assess symptomatic safety and arrange appropriate clinical review. The source is inconsistent about diarrhoea, so absence of diarrhoea is not a required discriminator.
