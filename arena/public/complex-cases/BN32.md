# BN32 — Discharge planning with differing accounts of home support


Task

Audience: a clinician. Use 350–500 words in English, including headings. Assess the supplied records at the stated decision point. Give brief clinical justifications, not a private reasoning trace.
Use these five sections:
1. Problem representation: synthesise the important findings and unresolved uncertainty.
2. Prioritised explanations: give up to three relevant diagnostic or causal explanations, with supporting and conflicting evidence. Do not force three if fewer are defensible.
3. Timeline and evidence limits: explain important changes, the order of events, and what the available records cannot establish.
4. Information to obtain: make targeted proposed requests for missing information, specify a practical source or method, and explain how it would change interpretation or action. Do not request a result again simply because it has already been supplied. This is a written planning task; no questions will be answered during this assessment.
5. Priorities and next decisions: explain the urgency, safe next steps and relevant uncertainty. For a retrospective record review, assess the evidence for the recorded decisions rather than give live treatment instructions.
Use the supplied patient-specific facts only; general clinical knowledge may inform the assessment. Keep observations, hypotheses and proposals distinct. Unreported information is unknown, not normal. Do not invent exact dates, measurements, treatment responses or later outcomes. Do not provide rubric scores.


Case input

A man was admitted to the Frailty Assessment Unit. The admission social history records a once-daily package of care for medication prompts.

Nursing staff handed the patient over to the medical team on the basis that he was deaf. Staff who had interacted with him said he could lip read sufficiently if spoken to slowly and clearly, but that he was also a BSL user. The phrase 'lip reads' was documented multiple times in the clerk-in document, although the BSL user box was not ticked on the front of the admission booklet. How his lip-reading ability was assessed could not be ascertained from the clerk-in, but it was accepted by staff as sufficient for communication.

On day 3 the patient was seen on the ward round by a Consultant Geriatrician and a Resident Doctor. After that consultation it was decided that he was ready for home, to be discharged with his existing package of care. This decision was based on the patient supposedly lip reading what staff were saying and replying with nodding for yes and shaking his head for no; he agreed to most questions asked of him.

A resident doctor returned to the patient after the ward round to confirm some answers using basic British Sign Language. The Consultant had asked whether his package of care was three times a day and included washing and meals, and the patient had nodded in agreement; when asked with BSL this was found to be incorrect.

Through basic BSL it was found that the package of care was only once a day and that he had no family close by to regularly support him. Basic BSL was also used to confirm whether he currently had any pain and whether he had other questions at that time. After this consultation and discussion with the team, the resident doctor informed the patient that he would not be discharged that day.

When past correspondence was reviewed on the online clinical portal, recent clinic letters stated that the patient required a BSL interpreter for all consultations. This was raised with the Consultant, who said that lip reading was sufficient and no BSL interpreter was required.

During admission, staff had tried to use written materials to communicate with the patient, despite online clinic letters recording that he struggled to read written English and could not effectively write due to a severe bilateral tremor. He also had several ophthalmology letters stating poor eyesight due to chronic ophthalmological conditions, further contra-indicating written media. The patient conveyed that he preferred not to communicate in writing, yet staff persisted in attempting that mode.

Up until this point (day 3), at no time during admission had a professional interpreter been offered or requested. The medical team contacted the patient's next of kin (a fellow BSL user, although not deaf), who usually took on the role of interpreting for the patient; over the phone he confirmed that the patient was a BSL user.

Sign language, not spoken or written English, was the patient's primary form of communication.

At this decision point, no professional interpreter had been requested or offered during the first three days of admission, and the reliability of lip-reading had not been established. Basic BSL communication on day 3 indicated once-daily contact and no nearby family support. The full type and scope of home support had not been established through a professional-interpreter-assisted discussion. The basic BSL account conflicts with the account obtained through nodding during the ward round.
