Case input

A toddler was referred to an ophthalmology department with persistent unilateral conjunctival hyperaemia and progressive thickening of the nasal conjunctiva in the left eye, present for approximately 3 months before referral.

Before referral, the child had been evaluated at another institution and treated empirically with multiple courses of topical corticosteroids, topical antibiotics and antihistamine therapy, without clinical improvement.

Examination at presentation showed localised hyperaemic thickening of the nasal bulbar conjunctiva of the left eye. The remainder of the ocular examination was unremarkable, with no corneal involvement, intraocular abnormalities or impaired ocular motility.

The child was born at term after an uncomplicated pregnancy and normal perinatal course. Medical history was otherwise unremarkable, with no family history of ocular disorders, malignancy or known genetic syndromes.

An incisional biopsy was performed because of persistent lesion progression despite prolonged medical treatment. Histopathology showed an inflammatory infiltrate, predominantly lymphocytes with rare plasma cells, without evidence of neoplastic proliferation.

Despite the initial reassuring histological findings, progressive worsening of the conjunctival lesion was observed over the following months of follow-up.

Approximately 5 months after the biopsy, the patient returned for re-evaluation because of further lesion enlargement. Additional topical corticosteroid therapy was prescribed; no clinical response was observed. The lesion increased in size and extended toward the nasal limbus, prompting surgical intervention.

At initial presentation, the persistent unilateral hyperaemia with focal conjunctival thickening raised suspicion for chronic inflammatory conditions, including allergic conjunctivitis, chronic infectious conjunctivitis and inflammatory pseudotumour. Lack of response to prolonged topical corticosteroids, antibiotics and antihistamines progressively reduced the likelihood of these diagnoses.

Following the first biopsy, which showed only inflammatory infiltrate without evidence of neoplasia, a reactive inflammatory process, nodular fasciitis, remained the leading diagnostic hypothesis as recorded by the authors. Continued lesion progression despite treatment and a progressive increase in tissue volume raised concerns regarding an underlying proliferative lesion. No further tissue, immunohistochemical or molecular results are available at this decision point.
