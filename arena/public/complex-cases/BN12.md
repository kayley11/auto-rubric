A man in his 60s with a 14-month history of multifactorial dizziness — documented left posterior canal BPPV (confirmed twice, responsive to Epley), orthostatic dysregulation on Schellong testing, and chronic sedative-hypnotic polypharmacy (levomepromazine, ethyl loflazepate, suvorexant, clonazepam, plus intermittent flunitrazepam) with heavy episodic alcohol use — now collapses at 3 a.m. while fasting. Key new context: substantial weight loss (85→73 kg), casual hyperglycaemia (glucose 192 mg/dL) with HbA1c 5.9%, newly treated hypertriglyceridaemia and hyperuricaemia, and a documented pattern that prior vertigo episodes occurred during fasting. The central unresolved question is whether the early-morning collapses represent fasting hypoglycaemia (possibly reactive, within a glucose-intolerant state), orthostatic/sedative effects, or a combination. No glucose, blood pressure or medication-timing data exist for any episode.

## 2. Prioritised explanations

**Fasting-related hypoglycaemia in glucose intolerance.** Supporting: 3 a.m. timing during fasting; episode clustering with fasting; elevated casual glucose with only mildly raised HbA1c, a pattern compatible with impaired glucose handling with postprandial swings; significant weight loss and dyslipidaemia. Conflicting: no episode-linked glucose value exists; HbA1c 5.9% argues against overt diabetes, and hypoglycaemia in non-diabetic glucose intolerance is uncommon but plausible, especially with alcohol (inhibits gluconeogenesis).

**Orthostatic and sedative/alcohol-related collapse.** Supporting: documented orthostatic dysregulation; four sedating agents at night; prior fall from a loft possibly flunitrazepam-related; heavy rum/wine binges; collapse at 3 a.m. coincides with peak hypnotic effect. Conflicting: prior orthostatic findings were mild and compensated; this event occurred supine-adjacent at night rather than on standing (posture during collapse unknown).

**Recurrent BPPV.** Supported historically, but a 3 a.m. fasting collapse without documented positional vertigo fits poorly; BPPV cannot explain the fasting association.

## 3. Timeline and evidence limits

Sequence: initial multifactorial vertigo diagnosis → cardiology exclusion → partial response to ryokeijutsukanto → recurrent BPPV with Epley at 4 months → fall and weight loss at 8 months → hyperglycaemia, dyslipidaemia, hyperuricaemia at 9 months → non-vertiginous lightheadedness with fasting/standing at 11 months → fasting collapse at 14 months. Limits: no measurement of glucose, lying/standing blood pressure, or drug levels during any episode; alcohol intake around events unknown; the "all episodes during fasting" association is retrospectively reported and vulnerable to recall bias; the hypoglycaemia hypothesis remains unconfirmed at this decision point.

## 4. Information to obtain

- **Episode-linked glucose:** CGM, or timed capillary finger-prick glucose during typical 2–5 a.m. windows and at symptom onset over coming days. A value <70 mg/dL during symptoms would confirm hypoglycaemia and mandate metabolic workup (insulin, C-peptide, alcohol history correlation); normal values would redirect toward orthostatic/sedative causes.
- **Symptom diary with lying/standing blood pressure, medication timing and alcohol log** around each event, obtainable within days. This would disentangle sedative/alcohol contributions from metabolic ones and guide tapering priorities.

## 5. Priorities and next decisions

The recorded decision to pursue CGM is well justified: the fasting-timing pattern plus discordant casual glucose/HbA1c makes episode-linked glucose capture the single highest-yield step, and it is low-risk. Urgency is moderate — a 3 a.m. collapse with potential hypoglycaemia carries fall and injury risk (prior compression fractures, loft fall), so interim safety advice (avoid fasting overnight, bedtime snack, supervised rising, alcohol moderation) is warranted while awaiting confirmation. Sedative reduction remains advisable but previously declined; the documented plan reasonably prioritises establishing the metabolic diagnosis first, since a confirmed hypoglycaemia mechanism would change management substantially (dietary timing, glucose intolerance evaluation, endocrinology referral), whereas exclusion would refocus efforts on orthostatic and medication-related measures.
