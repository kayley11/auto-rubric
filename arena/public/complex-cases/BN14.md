Case input

An adolescent boy presented to the paediatric outpatient department with multiple transient episodes of loss of consciousness over the preceding 4 years. Both the frequency and the duration of these episodes increased progressively over this period. Their frequency rose from 1 to 2 per week to 3–4 per day, and the duration of each episode lengthened from 1 to 2 min to 2–3 min. Episodes were preceded by lethargy, nausea and sweating, were characterised by altered sensorium and unresponsiveness, and resolved after administration of glucose with restoration of consciousness, rather than remitting spontaneously. The family did not routinely seek medical attention for individual episodes until symptoms began to worsen in the month before presentation. At that time, he also developed headache of acute onset, mild-to-moderate intensity, intermittent and frontal in location, accompanied by forgetfulness, confusion and vomiting twice a day.

He came from a resource-limited rural community. His mother was in apparent good health. His father had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus 7 years earlier and was managed with lifestyle modification and diet alone. He had one sibling, a sister, with no significant medical history.

On detailed history, the family described recurrent episodes of faintness, myalgia, paraesthesia and unexplained irritability, along with unusual hunger and tachycardia occurring predominantly in the morning or after physical exertion. The myalgia was attributed to generalised muscular exertion during convulsive, seizure-like motor activity accompanying the more severe episodes. Electroencephalography was not performed, and a primary epileptic disorder was therefore not formally excluded.

Four years earlier, at the onset of these symptoms, he had been admitted to another tertiary care institute, where capillary/plasma glucose during symptomatic episodes ranged from 20 to 38 mg/dL and normalised promptly with intravenous glucose. The referral summary available to us records that a circulating insulin level was measured at that admission and reported as normal. However, the numerical value, the assay used, and whether the sample was drawn concurrently with a documented low glucose cannot be established, as the primary records from that institution are not retrievable. No other biochemical marker of hyperinsulinism (C-peptide, free fatty acids, beta-hydroxybutyrate or the glycaemic response to glucagon) was assessed at that admission. Abdominal ultrasonography and contrast-enhanced abdominal CT were also reported as normal, and he was discharged with a diagnosis of 'idiopathic intermittent hypoglycaemia'.

On the current presentation, he appeared lethargic and unwell but was easily arousable, oriented and haemodynamically stable, with normal skin colour. His height was 168 cm (SD score −0.39), weight 68.4 kg (SD score +0.72) and body mass index 24.2 kg/m² (SD score +0.96). By the revised Indian Academy of Pediatrics 2015 growth charts, which define overweight in 5–18 year-olds by the adult-equivalent body mass index cut-off of 23 rather than by an SD-score threshold, he was overweight at presentation. Vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.9 C (98.4°F), heart rate 100 beats/min, respiratory rate 20 breaths/min and blood pressure 100/67 mm Hg without orthostatic change. Systemic examination was otherwise unremarkable. The peripheries were cool. The abdomen was soft and non-tender with normal bowel sounds and no palpable mass.

During observation he again became symptomatic with headache. Capillary glucose at that time was 48 mg/dL, and his symptoms resolved promptly after intravenous dextrose. A bolus of 25% dextrose was administered for this acute symptomatic episode, with capillary glucose rising to 79 mg/dL and resolution of symptoms within 15 min. He was subsequently maintained on intravenous 10% dextrose saline infusion with 2 hourly glucose monitoring.
