Current findings include palpable purpura, scrotal pain, leg oedema, and new-onset proteinuria (PCR 0.99) with hematuria. CT shows small-bowel thickening and ascites. Laboratory data show albumin 2.6 g/dL, platelets 505,000/microlitre, and a positive *P. falciparum* rapid antigen test, though blood smears remain negative over two days. **Hypotheses**
1. **Systemic Vasculitis:** The combination of purpura, proteinuria, and bowel thickening suggests a vasculitic process, potentially triggered by infection or related to his underlying colitis.
2. **Malaria:** The positive antigen test suggests *P. falciparum*, though negative smears make active parasitemia uncertain.
3. **EBV-related Syndrome:** The initial working diagnosis of primary EBV infection may explain the prolonged febrile illness and liver abnormalities.

**Reconciliation**
The positive antigen test despite negative smears may indicate a false positive or low parasite density. The EBV findings may represent a coincidental infection or a misinterpretation of a chronic state. The new urinary findings and purpura suggest a systemic progression toward glomerulonephritis and vasculitis, which may be secondary to any of the above triggers.

**Proposed Actions**
*   **Diagnostic:** Perform a renal biopsy to characterize the proteinuria and a skin biopsy of the purpura to confirm vasculitis.
*   **Monitoring:** Serial urinalysis and creatinine monitoring to track renal function.
