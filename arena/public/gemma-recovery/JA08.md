## Case information actually supplied

A woman in her seventies with diabetes and hypertension, taking metformin and telmisartan, presents after chest discomfort, palpitations and presyncopal episodes earlier that morning. She is asymptomatic at triage. Pulse is 50 beats/min, blood pressure 110/70 mm Hg, respiratory rate 18/min and room-air oxygen saturation 98%. The initial ECG appears to have rapid, changing wide complexes in most leads. Closer inspection is reported to show a different organised pattern in lead III: regular P waves and regular QRS complexes with independent atrial and ventricular timing. There is no corrected tracing, confirmed artefact source or treatment outcome yet.

## Original task

Write 180–240 words for a clinician. Reconcile the measured pulse, current condition and lead-to-lead discrepancy. Compare a true tachyarrhythmia with recording artefact, and consider whether a clinically important underlying rhythm may still be present. State immediate bedside checks, confirmation priorities and safety considerations. Do not equate suspected artefact with a normal heart rhythm or invent the repeat ECG, electrolyte results or later device treatment. Do not prescribe a drug dose.

## Saved final answer

### Clinical Evaluation: Rhythm Analysis

The patient presents with a discrepancy between the triage pulse (50 bpm) and the ECG, which shows rapid wide complexes in most leads. This inconsistency, combined with the organized pattern of independent atrial and ventricular activity in lead III, suggests the wide complexes are likely recording artefact rather than a true tachyarrhythmia. A true ventricular tachycardia would typically align with a rapid pulse and often present uniformly across leads.

However, the findings in lead III hypothesize a third-degree atrioventricular block. This would reconcile the bradycardic pulse of 50 bpm with the patient's reported presyncope and palpitations. The artefact may be masking a clinically significant underlying bradyarrhythmia.

Immediate bedside checks must include a manual pulse palpation and a physical assessment for hemodynamic stability. Confirmation priorities require a repeat 12-lead ECG with optimized electrode placement and a review of the tracing for movement or electrical interference. Safety considerations focus on the risk of sudden cardiac arrest or symptomatic hypotension associated with complete heart block. Until the rhythm is confirmed, the patient requires continuous cardiac monitoring and immediate access to resuscitation equipment. The clinician must not assume the heart rhythm is normal simply because artefact is suspected; the priority is identifying the true underlying rhythm.
