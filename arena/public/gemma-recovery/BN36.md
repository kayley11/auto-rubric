### 1. Problem Representation
A man in his 50s with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) on acalabrutinib presents with a two-month history of pruritic, erythematous, scaly plaques on the trunk and limbs. Clinical examination reveals a systemic process involving the skin and peripheral nerves, characterized by infiltrated plaques with associated sensory loss, infiltrated earlobes, glove-pattern sensory loss in the hands, and thickening of the ulnar and common peroneal nerves. Nerve conduction studies confirm sensory neuropathy. Histopathology shows epithelioid granulomas with a lymphocytic rim and perivascular/periadnexal granulomatous infiltrates. The primary uncertainty is whether this represents an infectious process (specifically leprosy), a drug-induced reaction, or a manifestation of his underlying malignancy/immunocompromised state.

### 2. Prioritised Explanations
*   **Leprosy (Hansen’s Disease):** This is the most likely explanation. The combination of hypopigmented/erythematous plaques with sensory loss, thickened peripheral nerves (ulnar, peroneal), and earlobe infiltration is classic for leprosy. The histopathology showing epithelioid granulomas is consistent. However, the negative Fite-Faraco stains and negative slit-skin smears conflict with this, though these are common in paucibacillary forms.
*   **Sarcoidosis:** This can present with similar cutaneous granulomas and peripheral neuropathy (neurosarcoidosis). The histopathology (non-caseating granulomas) is compatible. However, the specific pattern of nerve thickening and the glove-pattern sensory loss are more characteristic of leprosy than sarcoidosis.
*   **Drug-induced Granulomatous Reaction:** Acalabrutinib or other medications could potentially trigger a granulomatous eruption. While this would explain the skin findings, it is less likely to cause the specific pattern of peripheral nerve thickening and sensory loss observed here.

### 3. Timeline and Evidence Limits
The patient was diagnosed with CLL six months ago and started acalabrutinib. The skin lesions appeared two months ago. There is a discrepancy between the patient's history (denying sensory deficits) and the objective examination (significant sensory loss and nerve thickening), suggesting the neurological deficit may have been present longer than the skin lesions or was unnoticed by the patient. The records lack a baseline neurological exam and a detailed travel/contact history.

### 4. Information to Obtain
*   **Detailed Exposure History:** Interview the patient regarding residence or travel to endemic areas for leprosy to increase the pre-test probability.
*   **Chest X-ray/CT:** To look for bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy or pulmonary granulomas, which would strongly support a diagnosis of sarcoidosis.
*   **PCR for *Mycobacterium leprae*:** Perform PCR on the skin biopsy tissue. This is more sensitive than Fite-Faraco staining and would definitively confirm or rule out leprosy in paucibacillary cases.

### 5. Priorities and Next Decisions
The priority is to differentiate between an infectious process (leprosy) and an inflammatory/neoplastic process (sarcoidosis or CLL-related). This is urgent because the treatment for leprosy (multidrug therapy) differs fundamentally from the immunosuppression used for sarcoidosis or the management of CLL. The next step is to pursue more sensitive microbiological testing (PCR) and systemic imaging to narrow the differential before initiating systemic therapy.
