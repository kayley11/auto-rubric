### Clinical Evaluation: Daytime Sleepiness

**Observed Facts**
A 24-year-old man presents with severe daytime sleepiness, characterized by unintended sleep episodes (falling asleep on transport and while reading) and prolonged sleep duration, rather than subjective fatigue. History includes cataplexy-like symptoms (hand weakness when laughing), hypnagogic hallucinations, and sleep paralysis. He uses buprenorphine, venlafaxine, and prazosin. Recent substance use includes ketamine (several times weekly) and alcohol (weekly). BMI is 21; neurological exam and selected labs are unremarkable.

**Hypotheses**
1. **Narcolepsy:** Suggested by the triad of excessive daytime sleepiness, cataplexy, and REM-dissociated phenomena.
2. **Substance-Induced Sleepiness:** Ketamine and alcohol use may contribute to sedation or disrupt sleep architecture.
3. **Medication Side Effects:** Buprenorphine, venlafaxine, or prazosin may exacerbate sleepiness, though reducing venlafaxine did not resolve the issue.

Psychiatric comorbidities (depression, anxiety) and current medications can confound the presentation by mimicking or masking hypersomnolence, but they do not explain the specific REM-related symptoms.

**Proposed Actions**
The next step is to obtain polysomnography and a multiple-sleep-latency test to objectively quantify sleep latency and REM onset. A safety discussion is required regarding the risks of falling asleep while commuting or performing tasks. Thyroid function tests should be ordered to exclude endocrine causes.

**Clinical Justification**
The presence of cataplexy and sleep paralysis strongly suggests a primary sleep disorder over simple sedation. Objective testing is necessary to differentiate narcolepsy from substance-induced or medication-related hypersomnolence.
