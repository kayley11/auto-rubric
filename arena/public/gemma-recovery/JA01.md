## Case information actually supplied

A 40-year-old woman undergoes left intraocular lens exchange for recurrent vitreous haemorrhage attributed to uveitis–glaucoma–hyphaema syndrome. She previously had congenital cataract surgery with pars plana vitrectomy, lensectomy and posterior capsulotomy at age 13. Preoperative visual acuity was 20/30 in the left eye and 20/20 in the right. A sulcus single-piece lens has been replaced by a three-piece lens in a reportedly uncomplicated 15-minute procedure. Topical and intracameral agents included lidocaine and phenylephrine; the full medication record is available for review. About ten minutes later she reports complete left-eye visual loss. At thirty minutes she has no light perception and a marked relative afferent pupillary defect. Intraocular pressure is 8 mm Hg; the cornea is clear. Dilated examination describes an attached, perfused retina without visible detachment, choroidal haemorrhage, optic nerve pallor or vascular occlusion. She has mild ipsilateral supraorbital headache and no reported migraine or neurological history. Neuroimaging has been ordered, but no result is available.

## Original task

Explain what the apparently preserved retinal appearance does and does not establish. Integrate the operative history and drug exposure without prematurely declaring a benign drug effect. Do not invent angiography, neuroimaging or recovery results, or provide a treatment dose.

## Saved final answer

### Clinical Case Summary: Acute Postoperative Vision Loss

A 40-year-old woman presents with sudden, complete vision loss (no light perception) and a marked RAPD in the left eye ten minutes after an uncomplicated IOL exchange. The primary urgent problem is an acute, profound loss of visual function following intraocular surgery.

The preserved retinal appearance, perfusion, and clear cornea exclude retinal detachment, massive choroidal hemorrhage, and gross vascular occlusion. However, this does not establish the integrity of the optic nerve or the functional status of the retinal cells.

Differential diagnoses include intraocular artery occlusion, ischemic optic neuropathy, or a rare adverse reaction to intracameral agents (lidocaine/phenylephrine). The operative history of UGHS and previous vitrectomy increases the complexity of the ocular anatomy, though the procedure was reportedly brief. The ipsilateral supraorbital headache may suggest a neurological event or a vascular complication.

Immediate assessment priorities must include the pending neuroimaging to rule out intracranial pathology and a detailed review of the medication record to evaluate potential drug-induced toxicity. While the procedure was short, the timing of the loss suggests a vascular or toxic insult. Distinguishing between a mechanical occlusion and a chemical or ischemic event is critical for management. Further evaluation of the optic nerve head and retinal nerve fiber layer is required to refine the hypothesis.
