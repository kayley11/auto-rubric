### 1. Problem Representation
A woman in her 30s initially presented with rapidly progressive quadriparesis and areflexia, clinically diagnosed as Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS). Following treatment with plasma exchange and IVIg, she showed minimal recovery. On day 31, she experienced an abrupt neurological deterioration characterized by right-leg paralysis, severe hypertension (200/120 mmHg), and a transition from areflexia to upper motor neuron signs (brisk reflexes, extensor plantar response). Neuroimaging revealed multifocal acute infarcts (ACA territories, corpus callosum, cerebellum) and MRA evidence of multifocal intracranial arterial narrowing (beading), accompanied by leptomeningeal enhancement. The central uncertainty is whether the initial presentation was a misdiagnosed CNS process or if a secondary vasculitic/thrombotic process emerged following the initial illness.

### 2. Prioritised Explanations
*   **Primary Angiitis of the Central Nervous System (PACNS):** Supported by multifocal infarcts in different vascular territories, MRA evidence of irregular arterial narrowing, leptomeningeal enhancement, and elevated CSF protein. The initial "GBS-like" presentation may have been an early manifestation of CNS vasculitis affecting descending tracts.
*   **Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome (RCVS):** Supported by the abrupt onset of neurological deficits, severe hypertension, and the "beading" appearance on MRA. However, the presence of leptomeningeal enhancement and the initial prolonged course of quadriparesis are less typical for RCVS.
*   **Systemic Vasculitis with CNS involvement:** Supported by the multifocal nature of the infarcts and MRA findings. However, negative ANA, ANCA, and the absence of systemic symptoms (fever, renal failure, or pulmonary involvement) make this less likely than a primary CNS process.

### 3. Timeline and Evidence Limits
The timeline shows a biphasic course: an initial phase of lower motor neuron-like weakness (days 1–23) followed by a sudden transition to upper motor neuron signs and focal ischemia (day 31). The records do not establish the nature of the arterial narrowing (e.g., whether it is inflammatory, vasoconstrictive, or thrombotic) due to the lack of catheter angiography or vessel-wall MRI. Furthermore, the lack of histopathology prevents a definitive diagnosis of vasculitis.

### 4. Information to Obtain
*   **Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA):** The gold standard for assessing intracranial vasculitis vs. RCVS; would clarify the morphology of the narrowing.
*   **High-resolution Vessel-Wall MRI:** To look for wall enhancement, which would strongly suggest inflammatory vasculitis over RCVS.
*   **Brain Biopsy:** To provide histopathological confirmation of vessel wall inflammation or necrosis.
*   **Repeat CSF Analysis (specifically for oligoclonal bands):** To further characterize the inflammatory nature of the CNS process.

### 5. Priorities and Next Decisions
The situation is a neurological emergency given the acute multifocal infarcts and severe hypertension. The priority is to differentiate between an inflammatory vasculitis (requiring aggressive immunosuppression) and RCVS (requiring blood pressure control and calcium channel blockers). The transition from areflexia to hyperreflexia suggests the initial diagnosis of GBS was likely incorrect, and the patient likely had a primary CNS process from onset. Immediate stabilization of blood pressure and urgent vascular imaging are the critical next steps.
