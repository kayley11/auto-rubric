1. One-sentence problem representation

An adult with rheumatoid arthritis on methotrexate and intermittent NSAIDs presents with bilateral ankle oedema, morning facial puffiness, and frothy urine unresponsive to recent furosemide, raising suspicion of nephrotic syndrome.

2. Up to three prioritised diagnostic possibilities

NSAID-induced nephrotic syndrome (minimal change disease or membranous nephropathy): supported by intermittent NSAID use, frothy urine, and oedema pattern; limitation is that temporal relationship is unconfirmed and no protein quantification exists. Secondary AA amyloidosis from chronic rheumatoid arthritis: supported by known RA; limitation is that disease duration and severity are unknown. Primary glomerular disease (e.g., idiopathic membranous nephropathy): possible but less specifically linked to the current history.

3. Prioritised investigations with brief purposes

Urine protein-to-creatinine ratio or 24-hour urine protein: to confirm and quantify proteinuria. Serum albumin and creatinine with eGFR: to assess nephrotic-range proteinuria and renal function. Blood pressure measurement: to screen for hypertensive or renal contribution. Serum lipid profile: supportive of nephrotic syndrome. Renal biopsy: consider if proteinuria persists after NSAID withdrawal.

4. Important missing information and the questions you would ask

Duration of oedema and frothy urine. Current blood pressure. Whether NSAID use preceded symptom onset. Duration and severity of rheumatoid arthritis. Any prior renal or urinalysis results.

5. Safety and next steps

Advise cessation of NSAIDs pending evaluation (conditional recommendation, not a confirmed finding). Arrange urgent urine and blood tests as listed. If nephrotic-range proteinuria is confirmed, refer to nephrology. Monitor for thromboembolic risk if confirmed. Reassess furosemide; it has not provided relief and may mask the underlying process.

- Nephrotic syndrome, possibly secondary amyloid A (AA) amyloidosis or membranous nephropathy related to rheumatoid arthritis: supported by the classic triad of dependent oedema, periorbital puffiness and frothy urine (suggesting heavy proteinuria); limitation — proteinuria is suspected, not confirmed, as no urine report is available.
- NSAID-induced or methotrexate-associated renal injury (e.g., tubulointerstitial nephritis, minimal change disease, glomerulonephritis): supported by intermittent NSAID exposure and methotrexate use; limitation — no creatinine or urinalysis results are available, and timing of drug exposure relative to symptom onset is unknown.
- Chronic kidney disease with fluid retention, possibly compounded by cardiac or hepatic causes: supported by oedema refractory to furosemide; limitation — no renal, cardiac or hepatic test results are supplied, and breathing is reportedly normal, arguing against overt heart failure.

3. Prioritised investigations
- Urine dipstick and urine protein-to-creatinine ratio: to confirm and quantify proteinuria, the key discriminator for nephrotic syndrome.
- Serum creatinine, electrolytes, eGFR and albumin: to assess renal function, hypoalbuminaemia and severity.
- Serum lipids and, if proteinuria confirmed, renal immunology (ANA, complement, hepatitis B/C, HIV, serum free light chains): to screen for causes including AA amyloidosis and immune-mediated disease.
- Renal ultrasound: to assess kidney size and exclude obstruction; ultimately renal biopsy may be needed if heavy proteinuria is confirmed.

4. Missing information and questions
- Duration and progression of swelling; weight change.
- Urine output, colour, and any haematuria.
- Blood pressure history and any prior urine or blood results.
- NSAID frequency and doses, methotrexate dose and duration, and furosemide start date.
- Symptoms of infection, rash, joint flare, breathlessness on exertion, and drug allergies.

5. Safety and next steps
- Advise the patient to seek urgent care if breathlessness, chest pain, reduced urine output or leg redness/pain develops — this is conditional advice, not a present finding.
- Suspend NSAIDs pending assessment (a medication-safety recommendation, not a new prescription).
- Arrange prompt urine and blood testing; furosemide has not helped, so reassessment of the underlying cause is needed before escalating diuretics.
