Fluid balance charts documented a cumulative positive balance of approximately 2.5 L over the first week despite a target intake restriction of 1–1.5 L/day, which the patient could not fully adhere to because of thirst. Serum potassium, magnesium, and phosphate were within normal limits, and renal function remained stable, arguing against a renal salt-wasting syndrome driven by tubular injury. Serum cortisol drawn 12 h after the last hydrocortisone dose was 3.1 μg/dL, and hydrocortisone was empirically doubled to 60 mg/day during the acute illness; thyroid function tests were unchanged. Hyponatremia was managed with a combination of strict fluid restriction, temporary discontinuation of desmopressin for 24 h followed by dose reduction to 240 μg/day, and cautious oral sodium chloride supplementation starting at 3 g/day. Serum sodium corrected by 6 mEq/L over the first 48 h and returned to 132 mEq/L by cycle 1 day 16. Neurological symptoms resolved completely, and no seizures or other serious complications occurred. Because of the severity of the episode, cycle 2 was delayed by 1 week and ifosfamide was reduced by 25% to 675 mg/m2, while cisplatin and etoposide doses were unchanged. Prophylactic measures for cycle 2 included a structured fluid balance protocol with twice-daily weights, a lower desmopressin dose, scheduled oral sodium chloride, and closer laboratory monitoring every other day during the first 2 weeks. During cycle 2, the nadir serum sodium was 128.6 mEq/L on day 8, with only mild fatigue and no hospitalization required. Cycle 3 was delivered at full ifosfamide dose with the same supportive measures, and the nadir serum sodium was 131.2 mEq/L. Restaging after three cycles showed a partial response by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1, and the patient subsequently proceeded to consolidative local therapy. The temporal relationship between ICE administration and the development of severe hypotonic hyponatremia, together with elevated urinary sodium and inappropriately concentrated urine despite positive fluid balance, supported a diagnosis of chemotherapy-associated hyponatremia with contributions from multiple mechanisms: syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion related to cisplatin and ifosfamide, transient renal tubular dysfunction, and iatrogenic potentiation from fixed-dose desmopressin in the setting of altered free-water clearance. This case highlights the importance of anticipating compounded hyponatremia risk when salvage chemotherapy is combined with chronic desmopressin therapy for central diabetes insipidus after craniopharyngioma surgery, and it demonstrates that with proactive dose individualization, structured fluid management, and multidisciplinary endocrine input, curative-intent salvage chemotherapy can be completed safely in this vulnerable population.
