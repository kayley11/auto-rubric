On physical examination, her blood pressure was 148/92 mmHg, pulse 78 beats per minute, respiratory rate 18 breaths per minute, and temperature 36.5°C. Body mass index was 27.4 kg/m². She was alert and oriented with intact cognition. Neurological examination revealed no focal deficits: cranial nerves were intact, muscle strength was 5/5 in all limbs, deep tendon reflexes were symmetric, and pathological reflexes were absent. The remainder of the systemic examination was unremarkable except for a crowded oropharynx.

Laboratory investigations revealed a fasting blood glucose of 3.1 mmol/L, with low fasting insulin and C-peptide levels, excluding insulinoma. Glycated hemoglobin was 5.2%. Thyroid function, liver and renal function tests, electrolytes, and complete blood count were within normal limits. An overnight polysomnography study confirmed moderate obstructive sleep apnea with an apnea-hypopnea index of 22.5 events per hour and a lowest oxygen saturation of 78%. A video-electroencephalogram during sleep captured one typical episode arising from non-rapid eye movement sleep, stage N2, without any epileptiform discharge interictally or ictally. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed mild chronic ischemic changes but no structural lesions. Chest radiography and abdominal ultrasound were unremarkable.

Based on the constellation of findings—episodes arising from NREM sleep, complex motor behaviors without epileptiform EEG correlates, amnesia for the events, and documented nocturnal hypoglycemia—the patient was diagnosed with confusional arousals, a subtype of parasomnia overlap, secondary to recurrent nocturnal hypoglycemia and aggravated by obstructive sleep apnea. The nocturnal hypoglycemia was attributed to irregular evening meals and chronic stress-related dietary patterns; further endocrinological evaluation did not identify an organic cause of hypoglycemia.

Management included dietary regularization with a bedtime complex carbohydrate snack, continuous positive airway pressure therapy for obstructive sleep apnea, and stress-reduction counseling. Antihypertensive therapy with irbesartan was continued. Safety measures, including bed rails and removal of sharp objects from the bedside, were implemented. The patient was not prescribed antiepileptic or sedative medications.

At follow-up three months later, the patient reported complete resolution of nocturnal episodes after initiation of CPAP therapy and dietary modification. Repeat fasting glucose was 4.8 mmol/L, and no further hypoglycemic episodes were documented. Her energy and concentration improved, and she regained 3 kg of body weight. Nocturnal polysomnography at follow-up demonstrated a reduced apnea-hypopnea index of 4.2 events per hour with improved oxygen saturation.

This case highlights that recurrent nocturnal hypoglycemia, a readily correctable metabolic disturbance, can precipitate confusional arousals in adults, and that coexisting obstructive sleep apnea may amplify the frequency and severity of parasomnic events. Clinicians evaluating adults with new-onset NREM parasomnias should systematically screen for metabolic precipitants, particularly hypoglycemia, and for sleep-disordered breathing, as treatment of these underlying factors may obviate the need for pharmacological intervention. Differentiation from nocturnal frontal lobe epilepsy is essential, and video-EEG polysomnography remains the cornerstone of accurate diagnosis.
