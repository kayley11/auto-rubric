On readmission, the patient reported persistent, severe, holocranial headache, photophobia, and new-onset nausea with two episodes of vomiting. She denied visual disturbances, limb weakness, sensory changes, seizures, or alterations in consciousness. General physical examination was unremarkable, and she was afebrile with stable vital signs. Neurological examination again revealed marked nuchal rigidity without focal deficits; fundoscopy was initially reported as normal. Given the refractory nature of her symptoms despite appropriate treatment for presumed viral lymphocytic meningitis, a broader diagnostic workup was initiated.

Repeat CSF analysis demonstrated persistent lymphocytic pleocytosis (WBC 210 cells/μL; 95% lymphocytes), elevated protein (112.4 mg/dL), and normal glucose. Repeat PCR panel for common viral and bacterial pathogens, including herpes simplex virus, varicella-zoster virus, and enterovirus, was again negative. Autoimmune and paraneoplastic antibody panels in serum and CSF were unremarkable. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain with gadolinium enhancement was performed, which revealed bilateral symmetric leptomeningeal enhancement involving the skull base and frontal regions, without parenchymal lesions or hydrocephalus.

Given the chronic, relapsing course and the characteristic MRI findings, the differential diagnosis was broadened to include chronic inflammatory meningitic syndromes. A detailed ophthalmological consultation was requested, and elevated intraocular pressure with early glaucomatous changes was documented. Further history-taking revealed that the patient had been using topical ophthalmic corticosteroid preparations intermittently for several months for allergic conjunctitis, raising suspicion of corticosteroid-induced intracranial hypertension. However, opening pressure on repeat lumbar puncture was within normal limits, and neuroimaging did not demonstrate features of intracranial hypertension.

Additional laboratory investigations, including serum angiotensin-converting enzyme, soluble interleukin-2 receptor, and chest imaging, were performed to evaluate for sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancy. Serum ACE level was mildly elevated, and subsequent CT of the chest revealed bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy. A diagnosis of neurosarcoidosis with isolated leptomeningeal involvement was considered most likely, and the patient was started on high-dose oral prednisone (60 mg/day) after multidisciplinary discussion. A diagnostic workup for alternative granulomatous diseases, including tuberculosis and fungal infections, was pursued in parallel and remained negative.

Within one week of high-dose corticosteroid therapy, the patient reported substantial improvement in headache and photophobia. Follow-up CSF analysis after three weeks showed a marked reduction in pleocytosis (WBC 35 cells/μL) and normalization of protein concentration. She was discharged with a tapering prednisone schedule and scheduled outpatient follow-up with neurology, ophthalmology, and pulmonology services. The final diagnosis was consistent with probable neurosarcoidosis presenting as chronic lymphocytic meningitis, highlighting the importance of considering granulomatous inflammatory causes in patients with refractory lymphocytic meningitis of undetermined etiology.
