## S07

[Trojan and colleagues, Frontiers in Immunology, 2026, DOI 10.3389/fimmu.2026.1960742](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2026.1960742/full). Licence: [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/).

<a id="s07-b0052"></a>
### Original block S07-B0052

> A 36-year-old woman presented with a 5-day history of low-grade fever and severe stabbing headaches. Her past medical history was unremarkable. Neurological examination was unremarkable except for marked meningeal signs. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the head revealed no abnormalities. CSF analysis demonstrated inflammatory findings with lymphocytic pleocytosis (183 cells/µL; 97.8% lymphocytes) and elevated protein concentration (83.3 mg/dL). CSF polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing did not identify an infectious etiology. Serological testing for tick-borne infections, including Borrelia burgdorferi, was negative. Based on the clinical presentation and initial laboratory findings, the patient was initially diagnosed with lymphocytic meningitis syndrome. She received intravenous dexamethasone (Dexaven) for 9 days, starting at 8 mg/day for 2 days, followed by 12 mg/day for 3 days, then tapered to 8 mg/day for 3 days and 4 mg/day on the final day, together with symptomatic treatment. A follow-up lumbar puncture showed partial improvement, with a WBC count of 130 cells/µL and protein concentration of 46.1 mg/dL. After 12 days of hospitalization, she was discharged in good condition on 11 March 2026.

<a id="s07-b0053"></a>
### Original block S07-B0053

> After discharge, the patient continued to experience headache and photophobia, resulting in two visits to the Emergency Department. Head CT performed during these visits showed no abnormalities. On 14 March 2026, three days after the initial discharge, she was readmitted because of severe orthostatic headache accompanied by upper-limb tremor and dizziness. Neurological examination showed neck stiffness and a positive Kernig sign. Brain MRI showed no abnormalities. Contrast-enhanced spinal MRI demonstrated disc herniation at L3–L4 and L4–L5, with possible nerve root compression. CSF analysis showed persistent lymphocytic pleocytosis (178 cells/µL; 97.7% lymphocytes) with a normal protein concentration (36.6 mg/dL).

<a id="s07-b0054"></a>
### Original block S07-B0054

> Samples were sent to the Laboratory of Neuroimmunology, II Department of Neurology, Institute of Psychiatry and Neurology in Warsaw for autoimmune antibody testing. Neuronal surface antibodies were tested using indirect immunofluorescence assay (IIFT) on transfected EU90 cells at a serum/CSF dilution of 1:10. Antibodies against NMDAR, AMPAR1/2, DPPX, LGI1, CASPR2, and GABABR were not detected. GFAP-IgG was also tested using IIFT on transfected EU90 cells at a dilution of 1:10 and was detected in both CSF and serum. In addition, the patient underwent extensive infectious and immunological investigations, including HIV testing, testing for Borrelia burgdorferi and tick-borne encephalitis, Treponema pallidum serology, amphiphysin antibodies, lymphocyte immunophenotyping, and repeated molecular testing for infectious pathogens (Table 1).

### Original block S07-B0070

Because of persistent hamstring muscle contractures initially considered to contribute to the positive Kernig sign, the patient received physiotherapy. Subsequent assessment suggested that the limitation of knee extension was attributable to muscular contractures rather than meningeal irritation. After 20 days, her clinical condition improved, and she was discharged in good condition on 3 April 2026. Prior to discharge, she underwent otolaryngological, psychiatric, and neurological consultations.

<a id="s07-b0116"></a>
### Original block S07-B0116

> The diagnostic interpretation of the GFAP-IgG results differed between the two cases. In Case 1, α-GFAP antibodies were detected in both CSF and serum using indirect immunofluorescence on transfected EU90 cells, supporting the diagnosis in the context of the compatible inflammatory CNS phenotype and extensive exclusion of alternative causes. In Case 2, GFAP-IgG was tested in both serum and CSF using the same methodology. A characteristic α-GFAP staining pattern was detected in serum and interpreted as positive/probable α-GFAP antibody positivity by the reference laboratory, whereas the CSF sample was negative. Thus, the diagnostic evidence was stronger in Case 1, in which GFAP-IgG was detected in CSF and serum, whereas Case 2 represents a less definitive presentation in which serum GFAP-IgG positivity was considered together with the inflammatory clinical phenotype, CSF pleocytosis and elevated protein concentration, meningeal enhancement on MRI, and extensive exclusion of infectious and other alternative diagnoses.

## S08

[Cai and colleagues, Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine, 2026, DOI 10.3389/fcvm.2026.1928392](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cardiovascular-medicine/articles/10.3389/fcvm.2026.1928392/full). Licence: [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/).

<a id="s08-b0062"></a>
### Original block S08-B0062

> A 52-year-old man presented with a 1-year history of persistent watery diarrhoea (3–4 episodes daily), accompanied by acid reflux, upper abdominal discomfort, and numbness in the distal extremities. Previous gastroenterological investigations, including upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, had led to diagnoses of bile reflux gastritis and a colonic lesion treated by endoscopic submucosal dissection. Histopathological examination of the resected lesion showed a sessile serrated lesion with dysplasia. His diarrhoea remained refractory to symptomatic treatment. During the preceding 6 months, he also developed progressive exertional dyspnoea, fatigue, and unintentional weight loss of 10 kg. His medical history included gout and nephrolithiasis. There was no known family history of amyloidosis.

<a id="s08-b0063"></a>
### Original block S08-B0063

> On examination, his blood pressure was 80/46 mmHg and heart rate was 52 beats/min, with no other specific cardiopulmonary or abdominal abnormalities. Baseline NT-proBNP was 887 pg/mL, hs-TnT was 23.9 ng/L, creatinine was 1.3 mg/dL, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was 62.7 mL/min/1.73 m2.

<a id="s08-b0064"></a>
### Original block S08-B0064

> Given the bradycardia, hypotension, progressive dyspnoea, and unexplained systemic symptoms, transthoracic echocardiography was performed. This showed symmetrically increased left ventricular wall thickness (interventricular septal of 14.1 mm), reduced LVEF (36%), and grade II diastolic dysfunction. Two-dimensional speckle-tracking analysis demonstrated relative apical sparing on longitudinal strain imaging (Figures 1A,B), raising strong suspicion for cardiac amyloidosis. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging reinforced this impression, showing diffuse circumferential left ventricular wall thickening, markedly elevated native myocardial T1 values, extracellular volume values ranging from 49.85% to 52.47%, and diffuse subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) affecting the left ventricle and interatrial septum (Figure 1C), findings highly suggestive of cardiac amyloidosis (10).

<a id="s08-b0068"></a>
### Original block S08-B0068

> In the evaluation of unexplained left ventricular hypertrophy, Fabry disease was also considered because of mildly reduced α-galactosidase A activity (1.70 μmol/L/h; reference range >2.0 μmol/L/h). However, subsequent GLA sequencing revealed no pathogenic variant, making classic Fabry disease very unlikely, although plasma lyso-Gb3 was not measured (11).

<a id="s08-b0070"></a>
### Original block S08-B0070

> Aetiological evaluation was then undertaken. No monoclonal gammopathy was detected on serum/urine immunofixation or serum free light-chain testing, effectively ruling out AL amyloidosis. 99mTc-PYP scintigraphy showed planar grade 1 myocardial uptake with a heart-to-contralateral lung ratio >1.42; overall, the result was interpreted as equivocal rather than diagnostic for transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis (12, 13).

<a id="s08-b0071"></a>
### Original block S08-B0071

> Given the patient's impaired cardiac function and the risks of sedation and bowel preparation, comprehensive gastrointestinal endoscopy was not performed; instead, a low-risk rectal biopsy was carried out and confirmed amyloid deposition (Congo red positive with apple-green birefringence under polarized light) (Figures 2A,B). The patient declined endomyocardial biopsy. As the rectal biopsy specimen yielded insufficient tissue for definitive amyloid subtyping, 18F-florbetapir PET/CT was undertaken as an adjunctive imaging study to map the distribution of suspected amyloid deposition and identify a practical extracardiac biopsy target. This showed tracer uptake in the myocardium (maximum standardized uptake value SUVmax 2.7) and throughout the skeletal musculature (e.g., right gluteal muscle SUVmax 1.7), with a myocardial-to-blood pool SUVmax ratio of 4.5 (Figure 3). Guided by these findings, biopsy of the right gluteal muscle was performed and demonstrated amyloid deposition on Congo red staining (Figures 2C,D).

<a id="s08-b0079"></a>
### Original block S08-B0079

> Whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous missense TTR variant, c.236C > A (p.Thr79Lys, historically designated Thr59Lys) (Figure 4A) (14–17). Although detailed genetic counselling was provided, the patient's asymptomatic adult son declined cascade genetic testing and long-term cardiac surveillance. To further confirm the presence of the mutant TTR protein in the amyloid deposits, LC-MS/MS was carried out on the amyloid-laden gluteal muscle tissue. This analysis identified TTR with 88.7% sequence coverage (12 unique peptides) (Figure 4B). A peptide spanning residues 69–90 (TSESGELHGLKTEEEFVEGIYK) was detected, which carries a lysine instead of threonine at position 79 (Figure 4C), directly demonstrating the presence of the variant protein in the amyloid deposits. Together with the genetic findings, these results confirmed the diagnosis of hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis with cardiac involvement.

<a id="s08-b0084"></a>
### Original block S08-B0084

> Tafamidis 61 mg once daily and dapagliflozin 10 mg once daily were continued throughout the 3-month follow-up period. While additional guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) was considered, beta-blockers were avoided given bradycardia and hypotension, and sacubitril/valsartan (ARNI), vericiguat and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) were not initiated due to persistent hypotension. At the 3-month follow-up visit, blood pressure was maintained at 100/60 mmHg and heart rate was 60 beats/min. Repeat laboratory testing yielded the following results: NT-proBNP 408 pg/mL, hs-TnT 12.1 ng/L, creatinine 1.27 mg/dL, eGFR 64.5 mL/min/1.73 m2. After three months of treatment, the patient exhibited substantial clinical improvement: diarrhoea frequency decreased from 3 to 4 episodes per day to 1–2 episodes per day, and exertional dyspnoea and fatigue improved. NYHA functional class improved from III to II. Follow-up echocardiography showed an increase in LVEF from 36% to 49%. Despite these objective and symptomatic improvements, the patient remained concerned about long-term disease progression and lifelong medication. A comprehensive chronological timeline of the patient's entire clinical course, from initial symptom onset to the 3-month follow-up, is detailed in Supplementary Table S1 and visually summarised in Supplementary Figure S1.

<a id="s08-b0093"></a>
### Original block S08-B0093

> This retrospective case report has several important limitations. First, this is a single-patient observation, precluding generalisable conclusions. Second, the follow-up duration was limited to three months; the observed LVEF improvement cannot be definitively attributed to tafamidis therapy, and long-term sustained benefit remains to be confirmed. Additionally, serial global longitudinal strain (GLS) data were not available at this early time point, precluding a more sensitive assessment of subclinical myocardial recovery. Third, no head-to-head comparison was performed between florbetapir-PET-guided skeletal-muscle biopsy and conventional biopsy strategies. Fourth, formal autonomic function testing, tilt-table testing, and heart rate variability analysis were not performed. Fifth, plasma lyso-Gb3 was not assayed, precluding complete exclusion of Fabry disease. Despite these limitations, the case generates a testable hypothesis: in patients with suspected ATTRv and non-diagnostic bone scintigraphy, amyloid-binding PET may have a useful role in selecting a lower-risk extracardiac biopsy target. This question warrants prospective study.
