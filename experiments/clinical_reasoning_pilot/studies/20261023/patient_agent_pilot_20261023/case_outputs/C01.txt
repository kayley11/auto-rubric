1. Problem representation

An adult with rheumatoid arthritis on methotrexate and intermittent NSAIDs presents with ankle swelling, morning facial puffiness, and frothy urine unresponsive to recent furosemide, raising concern for a nephrotic-range proteinuric process with secondary oedema.

2. Prioritised diagnostic possibilities

NSAID-associated minimal change disease or membranous nephropathy: NSAIDs are a well-recognised cause of nephrotic syndrome; frothy urine is suggestive of significant proteinuria. Limitation: no urinalysis or protein quantification has been performed yet.

Methotrexate-related renal injury: methotrexate can cause interstitial nephritis, though frothy urine is less typical of this mechanism. Limitation: temporal relationship to drug initiation is unclear.

Secondary amyloidosis from chronic rheumatoid arthritis: a recognised but less common cause of nephrotic syndrome in long-standing inflammatory disease. Limitation: RA duration and severity are not provided.

3. Prioritised investigations

Urinalysis with urine protein-to-creatinine ratio: to confirm and quantify proteinuria, distinguishing nephrotic from non-nephrotic range.

Serum creatinine, albumin, and electrolytes: to assess renal function and degree of hypoalbuminaemia.

Serum immunoglobulins, complement levels, ANA, and ANCA: to screen for secondary or autoimmune glomerulonephritis.

Renal ultrasound: to exclude structural or obstructive causes of oedema.

4. Missing information and questions

Duration and severity of rheumatoid arthritis; how long has the patient been on methotrexate and NSAIDs? Which specific NSAID, at what frequency and for how long? Any recent weight gain or progressive oedema? What is the blood pressure? Any prior history of renal disease or haematuria?

5. Safety and next steps

Hold NSAIDs pending renal assessment (this is a conditional recommendation, not a confirmed finding). Arrange urgent urinalysis and renal function bloods. If nephrotic-range proteinuria is confirmed, consider nephrology referral for possible renal biopsy. Breathing is currently normal; monitor for progression to pulmonary oedema, which would represent a change in status.

1. One-sentence problem representation

Bilateral ankle oedema with morning facial puffiness and frothy urine in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis on NSAIDs, raising concern for nephrotic-range proteinuria.

2. Up to three prioritised diagnostic possibilities

(a) NSAID-induced minimal change disease: intermittent NSAID use is a recognised cause of nephrotic syndrome; frothy urine and oedema pattern are consistent, but no urinalysis or protein quantification has been performed to confirm. (b) Secondary (AA) amyloidosis from chronic rheumatoid arthritis: can produce nephrotic syndrome; however, this typically requires years of poorly controlled disease and is unconfirmed here. (c) Diabetic or hypertensive nephropathy: no history of diabetes or hypertension is reported, making these less likely, but they remain unexcluded without basic investigations.

3. Prioritised investigations with brief purposes

(a) Urine dipstick and urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR): to confirm and quantify proteinuria. (b) Serum albumin, creatinine, and electrolytes: to assess hypoalbuminaemia, renal function, and volume status. (c) Blood pressure measurement: to exclude hypertensive contribution. (d) If proteinuria is confirmed, consider renal ultrasound and, depending on results, renal biopsy to distinguish minimal change disease from amyloid or other glomerulopathies.

4. Important missing information and the questions you would ask

Duration and trajectory of oedema; any recent weight gain; blood pressure readings; whether NSAID use correlates temporally with symptom onset; disease activity and duration of rheumatoid arthritis; any prior renal function or urinalysis results; presence of haematuria or reduced urine output.

5. Safety and next steps

The combination of frothy urine, bilateral oedema, and facial puffiness is highly suggestive of nephrotic syndrome but is not yet confirmed. The patient should be advised to seek urgent medical review for basic blood and urine testing. NSAID use should be reviewed with the prescribing clinician, as continued use may worsen renal injury if a glomerular process is present. If oedema worsens, breathing becomes difficult, or urine output falls, the patient should seek same-day assessment. Furosemide is unlikely to be effective if the primary mechanism is hypoalbuminaemia rather than volume overload.
